1  ·  What a star means

Only a few in every 100 restaurants
in the Guide have three stars

Michelin gives restaurants one, two or three stars, and each level is a different promise. Almost half of the restaurants in the Guide are recommended but get no star at all.

1,000 squares

Here is the whole Guide, shrunk to 1,000 squares. Each square stands for about 8 restaurants.

★★★ Exceptional cuisine

"Worth a special journey." Three-star restaurants fill just 21 squares, about one row of the grid.

★★ Excellent cooking

"Worth a detour." Two stars add 71 more squares.

★ High-quality cooking

"Worth a stop." One star is where most starred restaurants sit: 415 squares.

● Bib Gourmand, no star

The rest are recommended for good food at a fair price, but get no star. That's 493 squares, almost half the Guide.

2  ·  How a star is given

Secret inspectors decide, and about half
of the restaurants in the Guide never get a star

Restaurants cannot apply for a star. Anonymous inspectors eat there several times, pay their own bill and decide as a team. They judge only the food. This tree follows every restaurant in the dataset to where it ends up. Thicker branches hold more restaurants.

The 5 things inspectors judge

  1. Ingredient quality
  2. Cooking skill
  3. Chef's personality
  4. Value for money
  5. Same quality every visit

The Guide

Every restaurant starts in the same place: 7,709 restaurants the inspectors chose to include.

The first cut

Only 51% come away with a star. The rest get a Bib Gourmand: good food at a fair price, but no star.

But is that enough?

Stars do reward excellent cooking. But they shine mostly in Europe, mostly in a few rich cities, and mostly on the most expensive tables. Most of the world's kitchens are never even looked at. Can a prize that most of the world cannot win really tell us where the best food is?
